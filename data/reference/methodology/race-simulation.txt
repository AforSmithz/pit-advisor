The race simulation: what it models and what it does not

The forecast is not a classifier over finishing positions. It is a lap-by-lap simulation of the
race, run thousands of times, because the structure of a race is most of what determines the
outcome and a classifier has to learn that structure from a few hundred examples instead of
being told it.

Each path starts from a grid drawn from the qualifying-pace model, with known penalties applied.
Lap one is sampled from the empirical distribution of position change conditioned on grid slot
and circuit, because the start is its own event and is not predicted by race pace. From there
every lap samples a pace for each car from its rating, applies tyre degradation and the fuel
effect, and resolves overtaking: a car within the following gap is in dirty air and loses time
against its own clean pace, and a pass is sampled with a probability driven by the pace
difference and by how hard the circuit is to overtake at. Safety cars are sampled at a per-lap
rate estimated for the circuit, bunch the field and open the pit window; strategy responds.
Retirements are sampled from a hazard rather than assigned.

Paths are aggregated into a full finishing-position distribution, which is what produces win,
podium, points and head-to-head probabilities. Every one of them is a count over paths, so they
are all consistent with each other by construction.

Weather enters as scenario weights over dry, mixed and wet, taken from the forecast for the
circuit at session time. Each scenario is simulated separately and the results are blended by
those weights. A past prediction can be replayed against the forecast that was actually
available at the time, because forecasts are snapshotted when they are fetched.

The known weaknesses, which are measured rather than suspected. The simulation is shy at the
front and generous at the back: pole wins fewer paths than pole wins races, and cars starting
in the middle of the field score points in more paths than they do in reality. Two structural
causes are plausible and neither has been ruled out. The pace draw is a single spread applied
across the field, which is probably too wide at the sharp end, and the pass model uses one
slope for the whole sport when a circuit that is hard to overtake at is likely also a circuit
where a pace advantage converts into fewer passes. Separately, the tyre model represents a
stint rather than a compound: degradation is one slope per circuit and a stop resets tyre age,
so which compound is fitted is not represented.

None of these are hidden by the reported numbers. They are visible in the reliability curves
and in the per-slot diagnostic, which is the reason both are published.
